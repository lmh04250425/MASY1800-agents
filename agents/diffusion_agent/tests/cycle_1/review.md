# Cycle 1 review (v1.0)

All four v1.0 outputs passed the frozen validator. The general paragraph was the same in every case. The reasoning was not sound. This review checks each output against the course framework (C1, C2) and against the agent's own register.

## Preserved failure 1: wrong population, wrong chasm verdict

v1.0 placed agentic AI in the **early majority, past the chasm**. It got there by putting numbers from different populations side by side and comparing them all with Rogers' 16% threshold:

- 34-40% of U.S. *adults* who have *ever used* generative AI (D1, D2). That is a person-level measure of a different technology, not agents.
- 23% of McKinsey respondents whose organizations are scaling an agent *somewhere* (D5). That is a self-reported online sample of larger firms. The same source says that in any given function, no more than 10% are scaling agents. v1.0 left this out.
- 31% of *developers*, who are lead users (D8).

The representative business figure was not used at all. According to the Census Bureau, 19.8% of U.S. firms use *any* AI in any business function (D4). Agent use must be lower than that. Gartner placed AI agents at the *Peak of Inflated Expectations* in August 2025 (D6), which describes attention, not diffusion. C2 asks exactly this question: is the technology diffusing, or only receiving attention?

## Preserved failure 2: weak evidence set the application stage

- The application was called "early majority" partly because "56% of schools use AI in admissions; 82% by 2024" (D20). That figure is:
  - **stale** (September 2023);
  - from a **mixed population** (21% of respondents were private high schools);
  - based on **undisclosed sampling**;
  - mostly about **reviewing transcripts and letters**, not recruitment support or agents.
- FAQ **chatbots** (D11, D16, D17, D18) were counted as diffusion of **tool-using agents**. They are different things.
- "Relative advantage: High" rested on a vendor customer story's *expected* $800 saving per inquiry (D14). That is a projection, not an observation.
- None of the five characteristic ratings said whether it was observed or inferred.

## Preserved failure 3: the timing implication overstepped and ignored posture

- **Primary (SPS):** "Both have crossed, so SPS should move now ... begin an applicant-facing agent pilot during this admissions cycle." This applies the course's fast-follower rule ("adopt after it has crossed") to the wrong crossing. It also makes the adoption decision, which belongs to the Adoption specialist, and skips the comparison gate set by A3 and A4 (C3).
- **Contrast 2 (conservative college):** "Even a conservative college should plan adoption within 18 months to avoid falling behind." Posture barely changed the timing, and fear of falling behind replaced evidence.
- **Contrast 1 (aggressive provider):** "The window is closing." The urgency rests on the wrong crossing verdict.

## Preserved failure 4: the evidence probe accepted two of five weak claims

v1.0 rejected the UAE comparison (geographically narrow) and the developer comparison (industry-specific). But it said the stale 82% admissions figure and the 23% scaling figure "support the early-majority placement." It also agreed with the memo's conclusion. It did not flag the $800 projection.

## What worked

- The general paragraph stayed stable.
- Accelerators (standards, vendors) and inhibitors (trust, privacy) were identified.
- The SPS chatbot Violet and NYU's admissions bot were found.

## Revisions for v1.1 (same inputs; cycle 1 outputs not edited)

1. **Denominator rule.** Every adoption figure states its population, its measure (ever used, regular use, production, scaling), its date and its geography. The chasm test is applied separately to each population, and figures from different populations are never compared.
2. **Diffusion ladder.** Place each level separately: L1 generative AI among people; L2 AI and agentic AI among organizations; L3 the application (FAQ chatbots versus tool-using agents in admissions); L4 the peer group and the organization itself.
3. **Evidence grades.** A = representative and recent. B = credible but partial. C = vendor projection, self-selected, stale (older than about 18 months for a fast-moving technology), or off-population. Grade C evidence can never set a stage.
4. **Observed or inferred.** Each of the five characteristics is labelled observed or inferred, with its source.
5. **Attention-versus-diffusion check (C2).** Separate hype positions, vendor launches and forecasts from measured use, and name the driver mix (external promotion versus peer imitation).
6. **Timing rule.** Name which crossing the organization's posture should key on, give posture-specific triggers, and never make the go/no-go decision.
7. **Weak-claim field.** Name the weakest diffusion claim and explain why it fails.

This review was written by the same AI assistant that generated the outputs; it is not an independent grade.
